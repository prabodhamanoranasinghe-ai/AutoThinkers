Personal knowledge systems fail in a predictable way: people collect endlessly, then confuse storage with understanding.

If you are part of the AutoThinkers audience — researchers, indie hackers, analysts, writers — you probably already have notes. The problem is not capture. The problem is **structure that creates new claims**.

Lattice thinking is a method for connecting ideas so they can bear weight.

## What a lattice is (and is not)

A lattice is a network of concepts with explicit relationships:

- *supports*
- *contradicts*
- *specializes*
- *causes*
- *is an example of*

It is not a folder tree. It is not a dump of highlights. It is not a second brain that quietly becomes a junk drawer with better fonts.

The goal of a lattice is reusable judgment: when a new fact arrives, you know where it attaches — and what changes if it is true.

## Why most PKM collapses

Three failure modes dominate:

1. **Collector’s fallacy** — saving replaces thinking  
2. **Orphan notes** — no outgoing links, no questions  
3. **Premature ontology** — tagging everything before you know what matters  

Lattice thinking postpones taxonomy and prioritizes **load-bearing links**.

## The five-node starter lattice

Begin with five node types only:

1. **Question** — unresolved, valuable, specific  
2. **Claim** — a falsifiable statement  
3. **Evidence** — observation, citation, experiment  
4. **Model** — a reusable explanation  
5. **Decision** — a choice made under uncertainty  

Example from a product research lattice:

- **Question:** Why do power users churn after month two?  
- **Claim:** They outgrow our templates but cannot extend them.  
- **Evidence:** 11 exit interviews; 7 mention “hit a ceiling.”  
- **Model:** Tooling retention follows an extensibility curve.  
- **Decision:** Ship custom fields before another template pack.

Notice what happened: notes became a chain that can be argued with.

## How to grow the lattice without drowning

### Rule 1: Every new note must answer “attaches to what?”

If you cannot name a parent question, claim, or model, the note is probably entertainment.

### Rule 2: Prefer verbs over tags

Instead of tagging `#retention`, write:

> Evidence *supports* Claim: extensibility ceiling drives churn.

Relationships are the product.

### Rule 3: Keep a contradiction log

Insight concentrates where models break. Maintain a small list titled **“Things my model cannot explain.”** Visit it weekly.

### Rule 4: Promote only after friction

Do not turn every idea into a model. Promote a claim to a model only after it survives contact with at least two domains or datasets.

## A weekly synthesis ritual (45 minutes)

Use this exact sequence:

1. **10 min** — Review new captures; delete 30% ruthlessly  
2. **15 min** — Attach survivors to questions/claims with verbs  
3. **10 min** — Update one model that gained or lost support  
4. **10 min** — Write a 150-word “thinking memo” for future you  

The memo is mandatory. Without it, your lattice is a graph nobody reads — including you.

## Tools are secondary (but choose deliberately)

Any tool works if it supports:

- bidirectional links
- fast capture
- readable long-form notes
- export (so you are not hostage)

Obsidian, Notion, Logseq, plain Markdown — irrelevant compared to relational discipline.

If your tool makes linking annoying, you will stop linking. That is a product decision disguised as a personality flaw.

## Worked example: from article to lattice

Suppose you read an essay on attention budgets. A weak response is highlighting 20 sentences. A lattice response looks like this:

**Question:** What operating constraints actually improve judgment quality?  
**Claim:** Protecting two daily maker blocks outperforms optimizing task tools.  
**Evidence:** Author’s case study + your own calendar experiment next week.  
**Model:** Attention is capital with opening balances and leak rates.  
**Decision:** Pilot maker blocks Tue/Thu for two weeks; measure shipped artifacts.

You leave the article with a test, not a quote collection.

## Metrics that matter

Track these monthly:

- Number of active questions (aim for 3–7)  
- Claims with at least one linked evidence node  
- Decisions revisited after new evidence  
- Memos written  

Vanity metrics: total notes, streak days, plugin count.

## Closing

Lattice thinking is how niche experts compound. Collectors accumulate. Thinkers connect.

If you want a practical default: start tomorrow with one question, one claim, and one piece of evidence. Link them with a verb. Write a short memo. Repeat weekly until your notes start arguing back — that is when a knowledge system becomes a thinking partner.
