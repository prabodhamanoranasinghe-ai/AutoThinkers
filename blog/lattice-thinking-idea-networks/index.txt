17:T19e1,<p>Personal knowledge systems fail in a predictable way: people collect endlessly, then confuse storage with understanding.</p>
<p>If you are part of the AutoThinkers audience — researchers, indie hackers, analysts, writers — you probably already have notes. The problem is not capture. The problem is <strong>structure that creates new claims</strong>.</p>
<p>Lattice thinking is a method for connecting ideas so they can bear weight.</p>
<h2>What a lattice is (and is not)</h2>
<p>A lattice is a network of concepts with explicit relationships:</p>
<ul>
<li><em>supports</em></li>
<li><em>contradicts</em></li>
<li><em>specializes</em></li>
<li><em>causes</em></li>
<li><em>is an example of</em></li>
</ul>
<p>It is not a folder tree. It is not a dump of highlights. It is not a second brain that quietly becomes a junk drawer with better fonts.</p>
<p>The goal of a lattice is reusable judgment: when a new fact arrives, you know where it attaches — and what changes if it is true.</p>
<h2>Why most PKM collapses</h2>
<p>Three failure modes dominate:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Collector’s fallacy</strong> — saving replaces thinking</li>
<li><strong>Orphan notes</strong> — no outgoing links, no questions</li>
<li><strong>Premature ontology</strong> — tagging everything before you know what matters</li>
</ol>
<p>Lattice thinking postpones taxonomy and prioritizes <strong>load-bearing links</strong>.</p>
<h2>The five-node starter lattice</h2>
<p>Begin with five node types only:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Question</strong> — unresolved, valuable, specific</li>
<li><strong>Claim</strong> — a falsifiable statement</li>
<li><strong>Evidence</strong> — observation, citation, experiment</li>
<li><strong>Model</strong> — a reusable explanation</li>
<li><strong>Decision</strong> — a choice made under uncertainty</li>
</ol>
<p>Example from a product research lattice:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Question:</strong> Why do power users churn after month two?</li>
<li><strong>Claim:</strong> They outgrow our templates but cannot extend them.</li>
<li><strong>Evidence:</strong> 11 exit interviews; 7 mention “hit a ceiling.”</li>
<li><strong>Model:</strong> Tooling retention follows an extensibility curve.</li>
<li><strong>Decision:</strong> Ship custom fields before another template pack.</li>
</ul>
<p>Notice what happened: notes became a chain that can be argued with.</p>
<h2>How to grow the lattice without drowning</h2>
<h3>Rule 1: Every new note must answer “attaches to what?”</h3>
<p>If you cannot name a parent question, claim, or model, the note is probably entertainment.</p>
<h3>Rule 2: Prefer verbs over tags</h3>
<p>Instead of tagging <code>#retention</code>, write:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Evidence <em>supports</em> Claim: extensibility ceiling drives churn.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Relationships are the product.</p>
<h3>Rule 3: Keep a contradiction log</h3>
<p>Insight concentrates where models break. Maintain a small list titled <strong>“Things my model cannot explain.”</strong> Visit it weekly.</p>
<h3>Rule 4: Promote only after friction</h3>
<p>Do not turn every idea into a model. Promote a claim to a model only after it survives contact with at least two domains or datasets.</p>
<h2>A weekly synthesis ritual (45 minutes)</h2>
<p>Use this exact sequence:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>10 min</strong> — Review new captures; delete 30% ruthlessly</li>
<li><strong>15 min</strong> — Attach survivors to questions/claims with verbs</li>
<li><strong>10 min</strong> — Update one model that gained or lost support</li>
<li><strong>10 min</strong> — Write a 150-word “thinking memo” for future you</li>
</ol>
<p>The memo is mandatory. Without it, your lattice is a graph nobody reads — including you.</p>
<h2>Tools are secondary (but choose deliberately)</h2>
<p>Any tool works if it supports:</p>
<ul>
<li>bidirectional links</li>
<li>fast capture</li>
<li>readable long-form notes</li>
<li>export (so you are not hostage)</li>
</ul>
<p>Obsidian, Notion, Logseq, plain Markdown — irrelevant compared to relational discipline.</p>
<p>If your tool makes linking annoying, you will stop linking. That is a product decision disguised as a personality flaw.</p>
<h2>Worked example: from article to lattice</h2>
<p>Suppose you read an essay on attention budgets. A weak response is highlighting 20 sentences. A lattice response looks like this:</p>
<p><strong>Question:</strong> What operating constraints actually improve judgment quality?<br>
<strong>Claim:</strong> Protecting two daily maker blocks outperforms optimizing task tools.<br>
<strong>Evidence:</strong> Author’s case study + your own calendar experiment next week.<br>
<strong>Model:</strong> Attention is capital with opening balances and leak rates.<br>
<strong>Decision:</strong> Pilot maker blocks Tue/Thu for two weeks; measure shipped artifacts.</p>
<p>You leave the article with a test, not a quote collection.</p>
<h2>Metrics that matter</h2>
<p>Track these monthly:</p>
<ul>
<li>Number of active questions (aim for 3–7)</li>
<li>Claims with at least one linked evidence node</li>
<li>Decisions revisited after new evidence</li>
<li>Memos written</li>
</ul>
<p>Vanity metrics: total notes, streak days, plugin count.</p>
<h2>Closing</h2>
<p>Lattice thinking is how niche experts compound. Collectors accumulate. Thinkers connect.</p>
<p>If you want a practical default: start tomorrow with one question, one claim, and one piece of evidence. Link them with a verb. Write a short memo. Repeat weekly until your notes start arguing back — that is when a knowledge system becomes a thinking partner.</p>
